# Notes: international frameworks

Scope: the treaty layer, WIPO and UNESCO work, US free trade agreements, comparative counts, preservation bodies and cross-border hosting. This is not legal advice.

## 1. What the core treaties require, and how much room they leave

### Takeaway
The treaties set minimum protection (programs are literary works, life + 50 years, legal protection for technological locks) but say almost nothing specific about reverse engineering or preservation. They leave exceptions to national law, limited only by the "three-step test". The WIPO Copyright Treaty requires "adequate legal protection" against circumvention but does not dictate the US model; the strict "ban the act and the tools, with a closed list of exceptions" model comes from US trade agreements, not from WIPO.

### Cited Findings
- **Berne Convention (Paris Act 1971)** (treaty text) — [WIPO Lex, Berne](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/283698):
  - Art. 5(1) national treatment and Art. 5(2) no formalities: a foreign game is protected in each member as if local, without registration.
  - Art. 7(1): general term is life of the author + 50 years. Art. 7(2): cinematographic works may run 50 years from making available (or from making). Art. 7(3): anonymous and pseudonymous works 50 years from making available. Art. 7(6): members may grant longer terms. Art. 7(8): unless national law says otherwise, the term is governed by the country where protection is claimed and does not exceed the term in the country of origin (the "rule of the shorter term").
  - Art. 9(2) three-step test: members may permit reproduction "in certain special cases", provided it does not conflict with a normal exploitation of the work and does not unreasonably prejudice the author's legitimate interests.
- **TRIPS Agreement (WTO, 1994)** (treaty text) — [WTO, TRIPS text](https://www.wto.org/english/docs_e/legal_e/27-trips_04_e.htm):
  - Art. 9(1) incorporates Berne Arts. 1–21 (except moral rights, Art. 6bis) for every WTO member, so Berne minimums bind WTO members that never joined Berne.
  - Art. 10(1): computer programs, in source or object code, are protected as literary works under Berne.
  - Art. 12: where term is not calculated on a natural person's life (e.g. corporate works), it is at least 50 years from authorised publication (or from making, if unpublished within 50 years).
  - Art. 13: the three-step test, extended to all exclusive rights (not only reproduction). TRIPS has no anti-circumvention rule.
- **WIPO Copyright Treaty (WCT), adopted 20 December 1996, in force 6 March 2002** (treaty text) — [WIPO, WCT](https://www.wipo.int/treaties/en/ip/wct/):
  - Art. 4: computer programs protected as literary works within Berne Art. 2, whatever the mode or form of expression.
  - Art. 10(1)–(2): three-step test for limitations and exceptions. The Agreed Statement concerning Art. 10 says the article lets parties "carry forward and appropriately extend into the digital environment" existing Berne-compatible exceptions and "devise new exceptions and limitations that are appropriate in the digital network environment" — [WCT Agreed Statements](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/295456).
  - Art. 11: parties "shall provide adequate legal protection and effective legal remedies against the circumvention of effective technological measures" used by authors "in connection with the exercise of their rights" and that restrict acts "not authorized ... or permitted by law". The words "permitted by law" leave room to allow circumvention for uses that an exception already permits. The text does not mention devices or trafficking.
  - Art. 12: rights management information.
- **WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty (WPPT), 1996, in force 20 May 2002** (treaty text) — [WIPO, WPPT](https://www.wipo.int/treaties/en/ip/wppt/): Art. 16 three-step test for performers' and producers' rights; Art. 18 anti-circumvention (mirrors WCT Art. 11); Art. 19 rights management information. Relevance to games: recorded music and voice performances inside a game.
- **WIPO's own 2026 reading of the framework:** the SCCR Chair's informal document for SCCR/49 (13 October 2026) states that Berne, the WCT, the WPPT, the Beijing Treaty and the Marrakesh Treaty "allow Member States to adopt well-crafted limitations and exceptions in their national laws, consistent with the three-step test" — [SCCR/49/5](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_49/sccr_49_5.pdf).
- **WCT membership:** the WIPO Lex contracting-parties page lists 119 parties. Parties include China (in force 9 June 2007), India (25 December 2018), Indonesia (6 March 2002), Malaysia (27 December 2012), Nigeria (4 January 2018), Philippines (4 October 2002), Thailand (13 October 2022), Türkiye (28 November 2008), Viet Nam (17 February 2022). Israel, Kenya and South Africa have signed but not ratified. Not listed at all: Brazil, Pakistan, Egypt, Iran, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Saudi Arabia. Taiwan cannot join WIPO treaties (not a WIPO member) but is a WTO member — [WIPO Lex, WCT contracting parties](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/treaties/ShowResults?search_what=C&treaty_id=16).

### Inferences
- The only reverse-engineering-relevant obligation common to almost every country is that programs are literary works (TRIPS Art. 10 binds all WTO members). Whether decompiling, studying or running a program is allowed is a national choice tested against the three-step test.
- A site can say plainly: "International treaties do not forbid studying a program you own; they leave that to each country." It should not say treaties *guarantee* a right to reverse engineer; none does.
- Because the WCT is silent on devices and on the access/copy distinction, a WCT country can lawfully allow circumvention for a permitted use (Brazil, India and others have broad or purpose-linked rules). Strict models come from trade agreements (section 3).
- Brazil, Pakistan, Egypt, Bangladesh, Iran and Sri Lanka not being WCT parties means no treaty obliges them to have anti-circumvention law at all, although several have some domestic TPM provision anyway (not confirmed).

### Gaps
- The article numbers and wording marked "(treaty text)" match the WIPO and WTO pages. They are not confirmed word for word against the linked texts.
- Saudi Arabia's absence from the WCT list comes from the WIPO page and is not confirmed.
- The current Berne membership count (about 181–182) is not confirmed.

## 2. WIPO work on exceptions for libraries, archives and museums, and on video games

### Takeaway
WIPO has studied library and archive exceptions since 2008 and has a standing agenda item, but in October 2026 there is still no treaty or agreed text. The newest step (October 2026) is a Chair's informal document with a preservation definition that covers "migrating works" to new formats. WIPO's only dedicated video-game study (2013) found no country has settled how games are classified, and most treat them mainly as software.

### Cited Findings
- **Crews study, 2017 edition (SCCR/35/6, 2 November 2017):** covers all 191 WIPO members; 161 have at least one library or archive exception; 28 have none and 2 have no copyright statute. Counts: preservation copies 102 countries; replacement copies 98; copies for users 105; making available on dedicated terminals 34; anti-circumvention exemption for libraries 53 — [SCCR/35/6](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_35/sccr_35_6.docx). Earlier editions: 2008 (149 of 184 members), 2014 (73 countries), 2015 (188 members) (same source). Many charts note that computer programs are excluded from library copying exceptions in a given country (e.g. "Computer programs are excluded (Art. 73(2))") (same source).
- The Crews study notes countries copy neighbours and trade partners when drafting exceptions; fair use statutes noted in Israel, Liberia, Republic of Korea, Sri Lanka and the US — [SCCR/35/6](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_35/sccr_35_6.docx).
- **SCCR/47 (Geneva, 1–5 December 2025):** documents SCCR/47/5 (Proposal on Limitations and Exceptions), SCCR/47/8 (Text proposed towards an appropriate international legal instrument or instruments), SCCR/47/9 and SCCR/47/10 (updated objectives and principles). The Committee "will work toward finding convergences in line with the 2012 Mandate and the Work Program adopted by the Committee in 2023" — [SCCR/47 Summary by the Chair](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_47/sccr_47_summary.pdf). SCCR/47/10 urges states to consider adding museums and other non-profits to these exceptions (search snippet) — [SCCR/47/10](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_47/sccr_47_10.docx).
- **SCCR/48 (Geneva, 18–22 May 2026):** Crews, Baloyi, Pantalony and Sutton presented the *Toolkit on Access to Copyrighted Works in the Collections of Cultural Heritage Institutions* (SCCR/48/5); comments invited by 1 June 2026; WIPO Legal Counsel briefed on possible forms of legal instrument; the Chair committed to an informal document on preservation, other disabilities and education. The topics stay on the SCCR/49 agenda — [SCCR/48 Summary by the Chair](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_48/sccr_48_summary.pdf).
- The access toolkit (SCCR/48/5, dated 27 April 2026) says it is not normative and does not reflect member state views. It notes born-digital works "may be subject to technical protection measures whose very purpose is to limit access" and that access "can depend on having the required software and systems, which are usually subject to licensing terms"; it lists "circumvention of technological protection measures" as a right an access policy may affect. It has no video-game-specific section — [SCCR/48/5](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_48/sccr_48_5.pdf).
- **SCCR/49/5 (13 October 2026), for SCCR/49 on 30 November – 4 December 2026:** defines preservation as "maintaining, stabilizing, restoring or migrating works, whether published or unpublished, ... including the conversion of analog to digital formats and migration to stable technological formats"; proposes letting cultural heritage institutions copy works for preservation or replacement "in analog or digital formats, and during technological migration"; restates the three-step test; says it "does not promote any specific type of legal instrument" — [SCCR/49/5](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_49/sccr_49_5.pdf).
- **WIPO 2013 study "The Legal Status of Video Games: Comparative Analysis in National Approaches"** (Andy Ramos, Laura López, Anxo Rodríguez, Tim Meng, Stan Abrams; CC BY 3.0 IGO) — [WIPO publication page](https://www.wipo.int/publications/en/details.jsp?id=4130); [PDF](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/pubdocs/en/wipo_report_cr_vg.pdf):
  - "none has clarified or offered definitive answers to the question of video game classification".
  - Mainly computer programs: Argentina, Canada, China, Israel, Italy, Russia, Singapore, Spain, Uruguay. Distributive (each element protected by its own nature): Belgium, Brazil, Denmark, Egypt, France, Germany, India, Japan, South Africa, Sweden, US. Mainly audiovisual: Kenya, Republic of Korea.
  - Conclusions (para. 293): "the majority of jurisdictions tend to protect these works of authorship as software"; the authors themselves favour a distributive approach and float a possible sui generis regime with a presumption of transfer to producers (para. 296). The study does not address preservation or reverse engineering exceptions.
- WIPO's video games page lists only this study and WIPO Magazine articles (2012, 2014) as its video game work — [WIPO, Video Games](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/copyright/activities/video_games).

### Inferences
- Classification matters for term and for exceptions: where a game counts as a computer program, software-specific rules (decompilation, back-up, and often exclusion from library exceptions) apply; where it is audiovisual, film rules on term (Berne Art. 7(2)) and authorship may apply. A site should say the answer varies by country.
- SCCR/49/5's "migration to stable technological formats" is the closest any WIPO text comes to describing emulation or reimplementation for preservation, but it is non-binding, limited to institutions, and still a draft.

### Gaps
- No WIPO study specifically on software interoperability or decompilation exceptions is known.
- Whether a post-2017 update of the Crews library study exists is not confirmed. The 2026 toolkits are a different product.
- SCCR/49 meets from 30 November to 4 December 2026. These notes do not cover its outcome.

## 3. Free trade agreements that export US-style anti-circumvention rules

### Takeaway
US FTAs turn the WCT's open "adequate protection" into a fixed model: ban the act of circumventing access controls and ban circumvention tools, with a closed list of exceptions. That list always includes reverse engineering for interoperability of an independently created program, but nothing general for preservation; libraries get only an "acquisition decisions" exception plus relief from criminal penalties. The same agreements push terms to life + 70. When the US left the TPP, the remaining CPTPP parties suspended exactly these provisions.

### Cited Findings
- **USMCA / CUSMA (in force 1 July 2020) Art. 20.66 (Technological Protection Measures)** — [USTR, USMCA Chapter 20](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/agreements/FTA/USMCA/Text/20-Intellectual-Property-Rights.pdf):
  - 20.66.1(a) liability for a person who "knowingly, or having reasonable grounds to know, circumvents without authority an effective technological measure that controls access"; 20.66.1(b) liability for making or trafficking in devices or services; criminal penalties for wilful commercial acts by anyone "other than a non-profit library, archive, educational institution, or public non-commercial broadcasting entity". Footnote 70: "a Party may treat a non-profit museum as a non-profit archive".
  - 20.66.3: a violation is "a separate cause of action, independent of any infringement".
  - 20.66.4 "Each Party shall confine exceptions and limitations ... to the following activities": (a) "non-infringing reverse engineering activities with regard to a lawfully obtained copy of a computer program, carried out in good faith with respect to particular elements of that computer program that have not been readily available to the person engaged in those activities, for the sole purpose of achieving interoperability of an independently created computer program with other programs"; (b) encryption research; (c) protecting minors; (d) authorised security testing; (e) personal-data privacy; (f) government; (g) "access by a nonprofit library, archive, or educational institution to a work ... not otherwise available to it, for the sole purpose of making acquisition decisions"; (h) further exceptions for a particular class of works when an adverse impact "is demonstrated by substantial evidence in a legislative, regulatory, or administrative proceeding" (the US triennial rulemaking model).
  - 20.66.5: the interoperability exception (4(a)) may also cover tools (1(b)); most others cover only the act.
  - Footnote 66: a party that already had compliant TPM law "may maintain its current scope of limitations, exceptions".
  - **Art. 20.62:** term not less than life + 70; for non-natural-person terms, not less than 75 years from first authorised publication (or 70 years from creation if unpublished within 25 years). Art. 20.89 gives a transition of 2.5 years for Art. 20.62(a).
- **Effect in Canada:** general term extended from life + 50 to life + 70 from 30 December 2022 (Bill C-19, Budget Implementation Act 2022 No. 1; Royal Assent 23 June 2022), not reviving works already in the public domain, and attributed to CUSMA — [University of Waterloo](https://uwaterloo.ca/copyright-at-waterloo/news/copyright-term-changes-life-plus-70-years); [Goodmans LLP](https://goodmans.ca/insights/article/canada-extends-basic-term-of-copyright-protection-to-life-of-author-plus-70-years-starting-december-30-2022). A Canadian commentary reads Art. 20.66 as allowing interoperability, security and government exceptions but no repair exception — [Lavery](https://www.lavery.ca/en/publications/5389-publication-display-PDF.html); a parliamentary witness on Bill C-294 (interoperability) said CUSMA's TPM section does not allow general non-infringing-use exceptions — [openparliament, C-294](https://op.alpheus.openparliament.ca/bills/44-1/C-294/?page=3).
- **Other US FTAs with the same model** (article numbers from EFF's comparative analysis by Gwen Hinze, undated): Jordan Art. 4(13); Singapore Art. 16.4(7); Chile Art. 17.7(5); CAFTA-DR Art. 15.5(7); Australia Art. 17.4(7); Morocco Art. 15.5(8); Bahrain Art. 14.4(7). Chile's text is the most flexible (requires actual knowledge; libraries can escape civil liability when acting in good faith); CAFTA, Morocco and Bahrain do not require knowing circumvention — [EFF, Seven lessons](https://www.eff.org/pages/seven-lessons-comparison-technological-protection-measure-provisions).
- **AUSFTA Art. 17.4.7** required an act-and-tools liability scheme with exceptions listed in 17.4.7(e)(i)–(vii) and further exceptions under (e)(viii) subject to 17.4.7(f); this drove an Australian parliamentary review of TPM exceptions — [Australian House of Representatives LACA committee report](https://aphref.aph.gov.au/house/committee/laca/protection/prelims.htm).
- **KORUS** (numbered 18.4.7 in the agreement) — one critique notes it reaches devices "promoted, advertised" for circumvention, wider than the DMCA's "marketed" — [Knowledge Ecology blog](https://blog.knowledgegov.org/?p=6640).
- **CPTPP suspended provisions:** Article 18.63 (term of protection, life + 70), Article 18.68 (TPMs), Article 18.69 (rights management information), Article 18.79 (encrypted signals), Article 18.82 with Annexes 18-E and 18-F (safe harbours), plus patent and data items — [Global Affairs Canada, CPTPP Annex II](https://www.international.gc.ca/trade-commerce/trade-agreements-accords-commerciaux/agr-acc/cptpp-ptpgp/annex2-annexe2.aspx?lang=eng); New Zealand: "will no longer be required to extend the term of copyright from 50 years to 70 years" and need not provide more extensive TPM protection — [NZ MFAT, CPTPP vs TPP](https://www.mfat.govt.nz/br/trade/free-trade-agreements/free-trade-agreements-in-force/cptpp/understanding-cptpp/cptpp-vs-tpp); [DFAT, CPTPP suspensions explained](https://www.dfat.gov.au/trade/agreements/in-force/cptpp/outcomes-documents/Pages/cptpp-suspensions-explained).
- **Japan** still extended its term from life + 50 to life + 70 on 30 December 2018, in the same law that approved TPP-11, although the TPP-11 term obligation was suspended — [bilaterals.org / Kyodo](https://bilaterals.org/japan-to-extend-copyright-period).
- **EU route, not US:** New Zealand committed in the EU–NZ FTA (in force 1 May 2024) to move to life + 70 by 1 May 2028 — [MinterEllison](https://minterellison.co.nz/insights/key-ip-impacts-from-the-eu-and-new-zealand-free-tr).

### Inferences
- In FTA countries (US, Canada, Mexico, Australia, Singapore, Chile, Korea, Peru, Colombia, Central America, Morocco, Bahrain, Oman, Jordan), reverse engineering for interoperability is usually protected against the TPM ban, but only for making an "independently created computer program" work with others. A clean-room reimplementation that reads the user's own files fits that wording better than tools that strip DRM to play or share the original.
- In the same countries, preservation by circumvention depends on a separate administrative or legislative exception (the 20.66.4(h) route), which most FTA countries have used sparingly. This is the main legal difference from countries outside the FTA network.
- The life + 70 spread is largely FTA-driven outside Europe; countries that never signed such deals (much of Asia, Africa, the Middle East) often stay at life + 50.

### Gaps
- The KORUS, AUSFTA, US–Singapore and US–Chile article numbers come from EFF and the Australian committee. They are not confirmed against the agreement texts.
- Whether New Zealand has passed the life + 70 implementing law is not confirmed.
- How many FTA partners actually adopted a rulemaking exception mechanism is not known.

## 4. Comparative overview: interoperability exceptions, preservation exceptions, terms

### Takeaway
No reliable worldwide count of decompilation/interoperability exceptions is known. Preservation exceptions are common (102 of 191 countries in 2017), but many exclude computer programs or require an institution. Terms run from life + 50 (the treaty floor) to life + 100 (Mexico), with corporate and audiovisual works often on separate clocks, and games' classification decides which clock applies.

### Cited Findings
- Preservation copies are allowed in 102 countries, replacement copies in 98, and a library TPM exemption exists in 53 (2017 data) — [Crews, SCCR/35/6](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_35/sccr_35_6.docx).
- Country charts in the same study often record "Computer programs are excluded" from library or education exceptions — [SCCR/35/6](https://www.wipo.int/edocs/mdocs/copyright/en/sccr_35/sccr_35_6.docx).
- Interoperability models: the EU decompilation model and the US FTA model both limit reverse engineering to interoperability of an independently created program — [USMCA Art. 20.66.4(a)](https://ustr.gov/sites/default/files/files/agreements/FTA/USMCA/Text/20-Intellectual-Property-Rights.pdf). Band's *Interfaces on Trial 3.0* (free download, PIJIP) covers US §1201 cases, Librarian of Congress interoperability exemptions and the CJEU's SAS Institute v. World Programming (2012) — [PIJIP announcement](https://wcl.american.edu/impact/initiatives-programs/pijip/news/pijip-counsel-jonathan-band-publishes-new-book-interfaces-on-trial-3-0-google-v-oracle-america-and-beyond); the 2011 volume (with Masanobu Katoh) covers the US, EU and Pacific Rim, including reverse-engineering permissibility — [MIT Press listing](https://mitpressbookstore.mit.edu/book/9780262538640).
- Terms: Berne floor life + 50 (Art. 7(1)); TRIPS corporate floor 50 years from publication (Art. 12); USMCA life + 70 and 75 years for corporate works (Art. 20.62) — sources in sections 1 and 3. Canada life + 70 from 30 December 2022; Japan life + 70 from 30 December 2018; New Zealand moving to life + 70 by 2028 — sources in section 3.
- **Mexico:** Ley Federal del Derecho de Autor Art. 29: economic rights last for the author's life plus 100 years (from the death of the last co-author for joint works); 100 years from publication where there is no human author's life to measure; Art. 29 was amended by a decree published 23 July 2003 — [WIPO Lex, Mexico LFDA](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/legislation/details/3094); [LFDA art. 29](https://mley.mx/LFDA/articulo/29/).

### Inferences
- For a typical 1990s PC game made by a company, the work-for-hire or corporate term often applies in the US and some other countries, while in Europe and many civil-law countries term follows the lives of the human authors. Either way no commercial game from the 1980s onward is near the public domain anywhere, so a site should not suggest that age makes old games free.
- Because classification is unsettled (2013 WIPO study), the same game may be treated as a program in one country and as an audiovisual work in another, which changes both term and which exceptions apply.

### Gaps
- No known source gives a worldwide count of countries with a decompilation or interoperability exception. A reasonable statement is "all EU/EEA states (Software Directive Art. 6) and most US FTA partners, plus several others (e.g. Australia, Singapore, Japan via its 2018 flexible provisions)". The non-EU items in it are not confirmed.
- No reliable count of preservation exceptions that explicitly cover software or games is known.
- Whether the 2003 Mexican decree created the life + 100 term or only revised it is not confirmed.

## 5. UNESCO's position on software heritage

### Takeaway
UNESCO treats software as heritage in three non-binding texts: the 2003 Charter (software named in scope), the 2015 Recommendation on documentary heritage, and the 2019 Paris Call on source code, backed by the 2017 UNESCO–Inria agreement on Software Heritage. None creates legal exceptions; they urge states to build them.

### Cited Findings
- **Charter on the Preservation of the Digital Heritage (UNESCO General Conference, 32nd session, October 2003):** Art. 1 scope includes "texts, databases, still and moving images, audio, graphics, software and web pages"; Art. 3 names "rapid obsolescence of the hardware and software which brings it to life" as a threat; Art. 8: "Member States need appropriate legal and institutional frameworks to secure the protection of their digital heritage", legal deposit "should embrace the digital heritage", and access to deposited material should be assured "without causing prejudice to their normal exploitation" (an echo of the three-step test); Art. 10 urges "hardware and software developers, creators, publishers, producers and distributors" to cooperate — [UNESCO Charter text (UNESDOC archive)](https://webarchive.unesco.org/web/20170127184223/http://unesdoc.unesco.org/images/0013/001311/131178e.pdf). It is a declaration of principles, not a treaty — [advocatetanmoy.com copy and commentary](https://advocatetanmoy.com/charter-on-the-preservation-of-the-digital-heritage/).
- **Recommendation concerning the preservation of, and access to, documentary heritage including in digital form:** adopted by the General Conference at its 38th session, Paris, 17 November 2015; legislative basis for the Memory of the World programme; states report every four years (38 reported in the first round) — [UNESCO Memory of the World](https://en.unesco.org/memoryoftheworld/recommendation2015); [Consolidated report 2019](https://webarchive.unesco.org/web/20200519225806/https://en.unesco.org/sites/default/files/2015_mow_recommendation_consolidated_report.pdf). IFLA urged states during drafting to amend copyright laws for heritage institutions — [IFLA](https://ifla.org/?p=109506).
- **UNESCO–Inria agreement** signed 3 April 2017 to preserve and share software source code through the Software Heritage archive (launched by Inria in 2016) — [Linux.com](https://www.linux.com/news/software-heritage-backed-unesco/); [Wikipedia, Software Heritage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_Heritage).
- **Paris Call: Software Source Code as Heritage for Sustainable Development** (from an expert meeting 6–7 November 2018, published February 2019) — [UNESDOC](https://unesdoc.unesco.org/ark:/48223/pf0000366715).

### Inferences
- These texts support the site's framing that software, including games, is cultural heritage, and that obsolescence of hardware and software is the threat. They do not give individuals any legal permission.
- Software Heritage archives published source code; it is not a home for decompiled commercial code, which matches the harness rule of never distributing decompiled code.

### Gaps
- The operative paragraphs of the 2015 Recommendation that mention copyright exceptions are not quoted here.
- Exact Paris Call date and signatories are from secondary sources.

## 6. International game preservation bodies and their positions on law

### Takeaway
The main bodies say the law is the biggest barrier, ask for exceptions for institutions, and avoid endorsing piracy. EFGAMP lobbied the EU and claims a partial win in the 2019 DSM Directive; the IGDA white paper (2009) remains the founding industry-side statement.

### Cited Findings
- **EFGAMP** (European Federation for Game Archives, Museums and Preservation Projects): June 2017 statement on the draft DSM Directive — [EFGAMP statement](https://efgamp.eu/the-statement-of-efgamp-on-the-dsm-draft-directive/); in October 2019 it said the adopted Directive (EU) 2019/790 met two of its requests — recognising games as cultural heritage and extending the teaching exception to cultural institutions — and that the Art. 6 preservation exception covers games and software (EFGAMP's own assessment) — [EFGAMP 2019](https://efgamp.eu/2019/10/22/winner-games-heritage-games-preservation-advanced-by-eu-copyright-directive/); August 2025 statement on the "Stop Destroying Videogames" European Citizens' Initiative: some hurdles removed, further legal measures needed, expertise offered to the Commission — [EFGAMP 2025](https://efgamp.eu/2025/08/06/efgamp-statement-on-stop-killing-videogames/).
- **IGDA Game Preservation SIG white paper** "Before It's Too Late: A Digital Game Preservation White Paper", *American Journal of Play* 2(2), 2009, pp. 139–166 (Monnens, Vowell, Ruggill, McAllister, Armstrong; ed. Lowood): argues for game preservation, identifies problems and proposes solutions — [University of Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/before-its-too-late-preserving-games-across-the-industry-academia/); [Cal Poly Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.calpoly.edu/lib_fac/107).
- US §1201 game preservation exemptions remain limited; a law review note argues for broader exemptions that recognise emulation — [Washington and Lee Law Review](https://scholarlycommons.law.wlu.edu/wlulr/vol82/iss2/8).

### Inferences
- These bodies speak for institutions. None of these sources addresses an individual studying their own copy, so a site should not claim their backing for individual reverse engineering.

### Gaps
- The IGDA white paper's legal section is not covered in full here.
- The Digital Preservation Coalition's legal position on games is not covered.

## 7. Cross-border issues: users, hosting, GitHub DMCA, geoblocking

### Takeaway
Copyright is territorial: what a person may do with their own copy is judged by the law where they act, while the host's obligations follow the host's law. GitHub applies the US DMCA process to every repository it hosts, whoever posted it, and geoblocks content per country for government requests.

### Cited Findings
- Berne Art. 5(1)–(2): protection is national and claimed under the law of the country where protection is sought — [WIPO Lex, Berne](https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/283698).
- GitHub's DMCA policy: the policy is written as a single US process with no carve-out for non-US users; to keep content down after a counter-notice the claimant must provide "a copy of a valid legal complaint" filed "in a court of competent jurisdiction"; circumvention (§1201) claims get review "by both technical and legal experts", and if validity is unclear "we will err on the side of the developer, and leave the content up"; GitHub will not disable a repository for a circumvention claim without first contacting the owner — [GitHub DMCA Takedown Policy](https://docs.github.com/en/site-policy/content-removal-policies/dmca-takedown-policy).
- Under the US statute a counter-notice must include consent to US federal court jurisdiction (for a non-US user, any district where the provider may be found) — [17 U.S.C. § 512(g)(3)(D), Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/512).
- GitHub government takedowns: it limits removal "to only the jurisdiction where the content is illegal" when possible, using geoblocking, and publishes the requests — [GitHub Transparency Center](https://transparencycenter.github.com/government); 2014 precedent: GitHub geoblocked specific pages in Russia to end a nationwide block — [TechCrunch 2014](https://techcrunch.com/2014/12/05/to-get-off-russias-blacklist-github-has-blocked-access-to-pages-that-highlight-suicide).
- GitHub also restricts some users for US trade sanctions, which is separate from copyright — [GitHub Transparency Center](https://transparencycenter.github.com/government).

### Inferences
- A project that hosts only original code (no game files, assets or decompiled code) gives a rights holder little to claim under §512, and a §1201 claim needs a circumvention tool. A reader in another country is still bound by their own national law when they run it on their own copy.
- Hosting in one country does not shield a user: the user's act of copying, decompiling or circumventing is governed by where they are (territoriality, Berne Art. 5(2)).
