# Report: presenting GameFrame as game preservation

GameFrame can honestly call its work game preservation. The claim holds only if the site shows the safeguards that make it true. **87% of classic US-released games are out of print** ([VGHF](https://gamehistory.org/study-explainer)). Games people own stop running when operating systems drop 16-bit, 32-bit or Intel code. Games still on sale, such as Banished, have no developer left to fix them. Law in the US, EU and UK protects studying a program you own and writing new code that works with its data. The same law does not protect sharing game files. Respected projects such as ScummVM, OpenRCT2 and DevilutionX all describe themselves as re-implementations or ports. Each one needs the player's own data. Each links to a store that sells the game. They rarely use the word "preservation" about themselves. Two risks shape the wording. The first is the industry argument that preservation is cover for piracy or a "free online arcade". The second is new, an October 2026 backlash against AI-driven decompilation. The site should therefore frame preservation as the *effect* of verified, documented work on the player's own copy. It should state its limits plainly. It should also face the AI concern head-on, because bit-exact verification is the clearest thing that sets GameFrame apart from cheap AI clones.

## Owned games break, and frozen games break first

The headline number comes from the July 2023 study by the Video Game History Foundation (VGHF) and the Software Preservation Network. It found that only **13% of US-released games from before 2010 are commercially available**. No five-year period rose above 20% ([VGHF explainer](https://gamehistory.org/study-explainer)). The platform figures are worse. The Commodore 64 sits at 4.5%. Almost all of that comes from one service, Antstream. Without it the figure would be 0.75%. The Game Boy library sits at 5.87%. Games from before 1985 sit under 3% ([VGHF explainer](https://gamehistory.org/study-explainer)). About 1,000 unique games left sale when the 3DS and Wii U eShops closed in March 2023 ([VGHF explainer](https://gamehistory.org/study-explainer); [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/reminder-the-wii-u-and-3ds-eshops-close-down-permanently-today)). No re-run of the study has been published, so the 2023 figure remains the standard one. One caution matters for honest wording. The 13% figure measures what you can legally buy and play today. It does not count games that are lost, since many survive on original media.

The second failure mode matters more for GameFrame. A game can exist, be owned, and still not run. **64-bit Windows does not run 16-bit programs** ([Microsoft KB 896458](https://support.microsoft.com/kb/896458)). **macOS Catalina removed 32-bit apps in 2019** ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/guide/32-bit-mac-apps/)). Windows 10 dropped the SafeDisc and older SecuROM drivers that games from about 2003 to 2008 relied on ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/au/report-games-with-securom-and-safedisc-drm-wont-run-under-windows-10)). Apple has said Rosetta 2 will be largely gone from macOS 28. It will keep a subset aimed at "older unmaintained gaming titles" ([TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-apple-macos-27-drops-intel-mac-support-rosetta/)). Apple has named the category itself. The site can cite that. Sources disagree on whether macOS 27 or macOS 28 is the cut-off, so the page should say "from macOS 28, by Apple's 2025 plan" and link the source.

Banished is the clearest case of a game still on sale but frozen. Shining Rock Software, a one-person studio, released it on 18 February 2014 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banished_(video_game))). A 2016 update promised a multi-platform version ([Shining Rock blog](https://shiningrocksoftware.com/?p=2987)). The last listed build is 1.0.7, which **GOG's build database dates to 14 September 2017** ([GOGDB](https://www.gogdb.org/product/1207660783/build/50512346553854639)). Any site text that says "last updated in 2016" should change to 2017. Whether Mac or Linux builds ever shipped is not confirmed, so the page should not claim either way. No dataset counts how many Steam or GOG games have had no update for five years or more. The page should present Banished as one example and should not invent a share.

Even rights holders end up working from the shipped game. Konami no longer had final code for Silent Hill 2 and 3, so the HD Collection was built from incomplete source ([Push Square](https://pushsquare.com/news/2012/05/silent_hill_hd_collection_ported_from_incomplete_code)). Square Enix's Tetsuya Nomura said Kingdom Hearts data "was lost" for its remaster ([Game Revolution](https://www.gamerevolution.com/news/5796-square-lost-kingdom-hearts-assets-forced-to-recreate-hd-version-from-scratch)). Media decay too. The Canadian Conservation Institute rates pressed DVDs and Blu-rays at 10 to 20 years and BD-R at 5 to 20 years ([CCI Notes 19/1](https://www.canada.ca/en/conservation-institute/services/conservation-preservation-publications/canadian-conservation-institute-notes/longevity-recordable-cds-dvds.html)). Tangled rights block re-release even when demand exists. No One Lives Forever still could not be bought legally at its 25th anniversary in 2025 ([Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/company/nightdive/)). These cases support one honest claim. The shipped binary is often the most complete record of how a game worked.

## Institutions keep the evidence, and community code keeps games running

The field splits its work. Institutions preserve objects and documents. The VGHF digital library opened on 30 January 2025 with more than 30,000 files, including over 1,500 out-of-print magazines, but no playable games ([Time Extension](https://timeextension.com/news/2025/01/the-video-game-history-foundation-digital-library-is-now-available-in-early-access)). It had nearly doubled by the end of 2025 ([VGHF](https://gamehistory.org/our-impact-in-2025/)). The Strong museum collects source code. Its 2014 Atari acquisition included code for dozens of games ([Giant Bomb](https://giantbomb.com/articles/a-museum-just-acquired-22-pallets-of-atari-history/1100-4905/)). That code is usually not public. Museums such as Berlin's Computerspielemuseum and Sheffield's National Videogame Museum give on-site play ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Videogame_Museum_(United_Kingdom))). Remote playable access for research is blocked in the US, as the next section explains.

Emulation fills part of the gap. MAME frames its work as documentation. It says its source code "serves as this documentation" of the hardware, and that playability mainly validates that record ([MAME README mirror](https://github.com/mahlemiut/wolfmame)). This is the closest existing model for GameFrame. A reimplementation documents one game's behaviour in code, and parity tests prove the record is accurate. Matching decompilations such as the Super Mario 64 project rebuild byte-identical binaries. They still need the original ROM for assets ([n64decomp/sm64](https://github.com/n64decomp/sm64)). Static recompilation through N64Recomp produced Zelda 64: Recompiled in 2024 ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/unofficial-pc-ports-of-n64-games-could-be-about-to-get-a-lot-easier)). GameFrame sits at a third point on that spectrum. Its output is readable new code that matches the original's behaviour exactly, with a structure of its own.

Community reimplementations are the closest peers. They share one rule. **Every project found requires the player's original data.** OpenRCT2 "requires original files of RollerCoaster Tycoon 2" and links to Steam and GOG ([GitHub](https://github.com/OpenRCT2/OpenRCT2)). DevilutionX needs DIABDAT.MPQ and points to GOG ([GitHub](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX)). The Force Engine says "a purchased copy of the original game is required" ([GitHub](https://github.com/luciusDXL/TheForceEngine)). Fallout 2 Community Edition says "you must own the game to play" ([GitHub](https://github.com/alexbatalov/fallout2-ce)). ScummVM calls itself "a complete rewrite of these games' executables" and supports over 325 games ([scummvm.org](https://www.scummvm.org/)). Its 2026.4.0 release marked the project's 25th anniversary ([scummvm.org](https://www.scummvm.org/)). None of these READMEs or front pages uses "preservation" as a stated aim. Journalists and institutions supply that word. dethrace comes closest in spirit. It describes itself as "an attempt to learn how" Carmageddon works ([GitHub](https://github.com/dethrace-labs/dethrace)).

Commercial recognition exists, but it is narrower than people often assume. OpenTTD has shipped on Steam since 2021. After Atari re-released Transport Tycoon Deluxe in March 2026, new Steam and GOG players must own TTD. The free website build continues. OpenTTD said it was "not 'pressured' by Atari" ([rubenwardy summary](https://blog.rubenwardy.com/2026/03/17/openttd-ttd-release/); [openttd.org](https://www.openttd.org/news/2026/03/19/steam-changes-update)). GOG lists DevilutionX as a free mod that requires Diablo ([GOG](https://www.gog.com/en/game/devilutionx)). EA released GPL source for several Command & Conquer games in February 2025. Players still buy the assets ([Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/EA-Open-Source-CnC-Red-Alert)). GOG's Preservation Program re-released about 100 classics with ongoing compatibility work ([GOG pressroom](https://www.gog.com/pressroom/gog-re-releases-100-improved-classic-games-as-part-of-the-new-gog-preservation-program)). Sources disagree on its launch date, October or November 2024, and most coverage says 13 November ([Gematsu](https://gematsu.com/2024/11/gog-preservation-program-announced-preserving-old-games-for-current-and-future-pc-setups)). The common model is that code may be open while game data stays a purchase. GameFrame fits that model exactly.

## The law protects study and new code, and never sharing the game

**United States.** The DMCA section 1201 exemptions in force from October 2024 to October 2027 let a person restore a server-dead game "for personal, local gameplay". They let libraries, archives and museums preserve games "in a playable form" only on their physical premises ([eCFR 201.40](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-37/chapter-II/subchapter-A/part-201/section-201.40)). In October 2024 the Copyright Office refused remote research access. It cited weak safeguards and a "significant risk" of recreational use. The Entertainment Software Association (ESA) opposed the request ([VGC](https://videogameschronicle.com/news/us-copyright-office-refuses-to-allow-remote-access-to-out-of-print-games); [VGHF](https://gamehistory.org/dmca-2024-statement/)). TechSpot counts it as the fourth refusal since 2015 ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/community/topics/us-copyright-office-denies-dmca-exemption-deals-blow-to-video-game-preservation-efforts.288839/)). The 2027 cycle opened on 9 June 2026, with petitions due 24 August 2026 ([Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/1201/2027/)). What preservation groups filed is not confirmed. For reverse engineering itself, **17 U.S.C. 1201(f) allows analysis for the interoperability of an independently created program** ([Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1201)). *Sega v. Accolade* (1992) held that disassembly was fair use when it was the only way to reach functional elements ([Copyright Office summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/segaenters-accolade-9thcir1992.pdf)). *Sony v. Connectix* (2000) held the same for intermediate copies made while building an emulator ([Copyright Office summary](https://www.copyright.gov/fair-use/summaries/sony-connectix-9thcir2000.pdf)).

**European Union.** The EU base is clearer. Directive 2009/24/EC lets a lawful user "observe, study or test" a program (Art. 5(3)). It allows decompilation where indispensable for interoperability (Art. 6). It voids contract terms that override these rights (Art. 8) ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2009/24/oj)). Check the directive's wording before quoting it. In *SAS v World Programming* (2012) the CJEU held that **functionality, programming languages and data file formats are not protected expression** ([SCL](https://www.scl.org/2451-sas-institute-inc-v-world-programming-ltd-report-and-analysis/)). *Top System* (2021) added decompilation to correct errors under Art. 5(1) ([Cuatrecasas](https://www.cuatrecasas.com/en/spain/article/decompilation-of-software-for-error-correction-cjeu-judgment-in-top-system)). Art. 6 also forbids using the information to make a program "substantially similar in expression". That rule is why GameFrame insists on new code. The Stop Destroying Videogames citizens' initiative gathered 1,294,188 verified signatures. On 16 June 2026 the Commission declined to legislate and offered to explore a voluntary industry code of conduct ([European Audiovisual Observatory](https://merlin.obs.coe.int/article/10576); [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/06/24/european-commission-stops-short-of-imposing-mandatory-requirements-following-citi-102n4yy)). That debate is about consumer law. It creates no new right to copy games.

**United Kingdom.** CDPA 1988 s.50B (decompilation) and s.50BA (observe, study and test) mirror the EU rules ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/50B)). The Stop Killing Games petition reached 189,887 signatures. The government said it had "no plans to amend UK consumer law" ([UK Parliament](https://petition.parliament.uk/petitions/702074)) and repeated that in the debate on 3 November 2025 ([Hansard](https://hansard.parliament.uk/commons/2025-11-03/debates/C603C07C-1BB5-41EF-A3C1-1AE1FEBBB95D/VideoGamesConsumerLaw)).

Across all three regions, **"abandonware" is not a legal category**. Copyright lasts whether or not a game is on sale. ScummVM's FAQ says the term "is not a proper legal concept in any country" ([ScummVM FAQ](https://docs.scummvm.org/en/latest/help/faq.html)). The takedowns show where the lines are. Nintendo targeted builds of the Super Mario 64 PC port that bundled the game's assets ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/nintendo-takes-action-against-mario-64-pc-port/)). Yuzu settled for $2.4 million over circumvention and anti-trafficking claims ([ChannelNews](https://www.channelnews.com.au/nintendo-settles-lawsuit-with-tropic-haze/)). The re3 and reVC projects for GTA III and Vice City needed the player's data and carried a no-piracy disclaimer. Take-Two still removed them and sued, because their code was a decompilation of the original executables ([GitHub DMCA](https://github.com/github/dmca/blob/master/2021/02/2021-02-19-take-two.md); [VGC](https://videogameschronicle.com/news/take-two-is-suing-the-creators-of-a-gta-3-and-vice-city-reverse-engineering-projects)). A settlement in principle was reported in 2023, with no public terms ([NME](https://www.nme.com/news/take-two-and-grand-theft-auto-modders-will-settle-legal-battle-3394677)). The lesson is that "bring your own data" is necessary but not sufficient. A project's code must be new, its process must be documented, and its target matters. A high-earning franchise that is still on sale attracts enforcement.

GameFrame has one structural advantage it should state. It distributes no output for any game. It ships a method and tools, tested only on its own sample programs. Per-game projects live in the user's own repositories under the same rules. The page must also say plainly that it is not legal advice.

## Respected projects lead with value, then state one rule plainly

Mission statements in the field are short and positive. The VGHF is "dedicated to preserving, celebrating, and teaching the history of video games" ([gamehistory.org](https://gamehistory.org/)). Software Heritage aims to "collect, preserve and share" source code as heritage ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_Heritage)). GOG's program uses the line "make games live forever" ([GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2024/11/gog-launch-their-preservation-program-to-make-games-live-forever-with-hundreds-of-classics-being-re-released)). Projects talk about modern systems, as in DevilutionX's "Diablo build for modern operating systems" ([GitHub](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX)). Two framings are in use. Institutions speak of history and study. Projects speak of playability on current machines. GameFrame can use both, as long as each sentence carries only one of them.

The legal statement follows a fixed pattern. One sentence near the top says the original files are required. Store links sit next to it, and freeware releases appear only where the rights holder allowed them. ScummVM states "a strict no-piracy policy" and refuses support when a copy is clearly not legal ([ScummVM FAQ](https://docs.scummvm.org/en/latest/help/faq.html)). The tone stays calm. Stop Killing Games uses "They kill games. We fight back." ([stopkillinggames.com](https://www.stopkillinggames.com/)). That works for a campaign, but a tool project that wants goodwill from developers should avoid it.

The vocabulary to avoid comes from the other side's arguments. In 2015 the ESA called hacking "an activity closely associated with piracy in the minds of the marketplace" ([EFF](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2015/04/videogame-publishers-no-preserving-abandoned-games-even-museums-and-archives)). In 2024 the Copyright Office accepted fears of a "free online arcade" ([VGC](https://videogameschronicle.com/news/us-copyright-office-refuses-to-allow-remote-access-to-out-of-print-games)). GOG's own "preservation" label drew user scepticism that it was marketing ([GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2024/11/gog-launch-their-preservation-program-to-make-games-live-forever-with-hundreds-of-classics-being-re-released)). Each finding points the same way. Concrete safeguards persuade, and broad claims invite doubt.

| Use | Avoid |
|---|---|
| your own copy, the copy you bought, original files | abandonware, ROMs, warez, free games |
| keep playable, run on current systems | crack, rip, unlock, bypass, hack |
| reimplementation, new code, rebuild | clone, copy of the game, recreation |
| document, record how it worked, study | "save" a game from its owner |
| out of print, no longer updated | dead, abandoned by greedy publishers |
| interoperability (in the legal section only) | fight, take back, liberate |

No credible source shows broad developer praise for reimplementations. Ron Gilbert's praise of ScummVM is documented only second-hand ([ScummVM news log](https://lists.scummvm.org/pipermail/scummvm-git-logs/2005-August/021431.html)). No on-record view from Chris Sawyer about OpenRCT2 was found. No Shining Rock statement about Un-Banished was found. The site should cite only specific, sourced cases, such as GOG listing DevilutionX or EA's GPL releases. It should never imply endorsement of Un-Banished. For Banished, the honest line is factual. The developer shipped a mod kit so players could "see how things are built" ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/banished-mod-support-is-on-the-way-says-developer)). The game is still on sale. The case study requires a bought copy and links to the stores.


## The AI concern is real, and verification is the answer

The sharpest critique of GameFrame is new. On 15 October 2026, 404 Media reported that decomp.dev, a long-running human decompilation community, was shutting down. Its admin said "the community aspect of decompilation has been broken" by AI, and that human-led projects can no longer be told apart from AI-driven ones ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/video-game-reverse-engineering-community-shuts-down-because-ai-has-made-it-too-easy/)). The same coverage links AI to "a cheap rip off of a video game in just a few hours". The admin also allowed that AI can help "with the right guidance, human input, and code reviews" ([404 Media](https://www.404media.co/video-game-reverse-engineering-community-shuts-down-because-ai-has-made-it-too-easy/)). Naavik's analysis names REA, a tool GameFrame uses, as a way for Claude to build mods end to end ([Naavik](https://naavik.substack.com/p/viral-game-mash-ups-what-ai-decompilation)). (search snippet)

The site should name the concern before critics do. GameFrame's answer is its structure. A vibe-coded clone imitates a game's surface. GameFrame requires the original game, runs its functions under emulation for ground truth, and fails a port that does not match bit for bit. It cites the original function on every item. It writes the findings down as documentation that people review. Academic work in 2026 already questions how faithful LLM decompilers are. One paper title is "When LLM Decompilers Recompile More and Preserve Less" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.05370)). (Only the title was seen.) The page can cite it as a reason GameFrame checks everything against the original. It should also credit the human communities whose methods it learned from. *Inferred:* bit-exact verification fits the "specific safeguards" the Copyright Office said were missing in 2024 better than any disclaimer does.

GameFrame should also be honest about what it cannot fix. Reimplementation helps only people who already own a copy. It does nothing for the 87% problem as a whole. It does not return games whose servers are gone unless the files survive. A page that admits this will read as more credible than one that claims to save gaming history.

## Suggested messaging and page outline

All copy below follows the house style. Each sentence is short and carries one idea. There are no colon reveals, no "X, not Y" endings, and no balanced pairs or lists of three.

**Home page hero.** Keep the name. Change the text and tagline.

> **Text:** Keep the games you own running on new computers.
> **Tagline:** A tested method for AI agents and people. It rebuilds a game in new code that behaves exactly like the original.

**New feature card (place it first).**

> **Title:** Game preservation
> **Details:** Many older games no longer run on current systems. Some are still on sale but have not been updated in years. This method works from the copy you bought. It records how the game works as it goes.
> **Link:** /preservation

**Revised "Within the law" card.**

> **Details:** You need your own copy of the game. GameFrame ships no game files. Each project writes new code from documented behaviour.

**Preservation page (`/preservation`).** Title it "Game preservation". Its description is "Why old games need care, and how GameFrame helps owners keep them playable."

| Section | What it says (draft copy) | Sources to link |
|---|---|---|

Two draft paragraphs show the tone for the hardest sections.

> **AI and reverse engineering.** AI tools can now read old game code quickly. That speed worries people who have done this work by hand for years. It also makes cheap imitations easy. GameFrame asks for more than speed. It runs the original game's code to get exact answers. The new code must give the same answers to the last bit. Every finding cites the place in the original it came from. A person reviews the work before it lands.

> **For developers and publishers.** We respect the people who made these games. GameFrame never ships your game or its assets. Every project needs a bought copy. Where your game is on sale, our guides point players to your store page. If you have a concern, please contact us. We will reply.

## Conclusion

The peer projects show that "preservation" is a word others apply to good reimplementation work, and projects rarely claim it for themselves. Regulators have already ruled that good intentions are not a safeguard. The October 2026 AI backlash means a vague claim from an AI-agent project would read as cover. GameFrame can claim the word only where its structure earns it. That structure is the player's own copy, no game material, bit-exact tests against the original, and documentation that outlives any one port. The most useful framing is MAME's. The code is the record of how the game worked, and playing it proves the record is right.

Two things follow. First, the Preservation page should state its limits as clearly as its aims, because modesty is the credibility signal this audience reads. Second, the verification story is GameFrame's best answer to the AI critique and should be the most prominent part of the page. That positions GameFrame as the careful option in a field worried about careless ones. It also gives preservation advocates something concrete to point to when the next exemption fight comes.
