# Notes: who preserves games, and where reimplementation fits

Research note, 21 October 2026. Primary sources were used where reachable. Several key questions are only partly covered, and the Gaps sections say where.

## Institutions: who collects what, and who gives playable access

### Takeaway
Institutions mostly preserve artefacts and documents: hardware, boxed media, magazines, press material, sometimes source code. Playable access is either on-site (museums) or blocked in law for remote research (US DMCA section 1201). The VGHF digital library (early access since 30 January 2025) offers documents only, not playable games.

### Cited Findings
- **Video Game History Foundation (VGHF).** Founded in 2017 by Frank Cifaldi. Its digital library opened in early access on 30 January 2025 at library.gamehistory.org, free to anyone. It had more than 30,000 curated files at launch, including more than 1,500 full-text searchable out-of-print magazines, plus development assets, artwork and promotional material. Launch collections included E3 directories, GamePro press CDs, FromSoftware promotional material, the Mark Flitman collection and the Cyan collection (video from the making of Myst). Phil Salvador led it as library director — [Time Extension](https://timeextension.com/news/2025/01/the-video-game-history-foundation-digital-library-is-now-available-in-early-access); [Game Developer press release](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/press-release/the-video-game-history-foundation-launches-its-digital-library-offering-one-of-a-kind-digital-archives-of-gaming-history)
- The VGHF's 2025 year-end recap says the library "nearly doubled in size since launch" — [VGHF, our impact in 2025](https://gamehistory.org/our-impact-in-2025/)
- **DMCA 2024 triennial ruling (US).** In October 2024 the Copyright Office refused a new exemption that would have let libraries and archives give remote access to out-of-print games for scholarly research. The Software Preservation Network asked for it, with VGHF support. The Register (Shira Perlmutter) found that proponents had not shown the use was non-infringing, and raised unresolved single-user and premises limits. The ESA argued that remote access could harm the market for re-releases. The VGHF said the ESA "would never support remote game access for research purposes under any conditions." TechSpot calls this the fourth refusal since 2015 — [VGHF statement](https://gamehistory.org/dmca-2024-statement/); [VGC](https://videogameschronicle.com/news/us-copyright-office-refuses-to-allow-remote-access-to-out-of-print-games); [TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/community/topics/us-copyright-office-denies-dmca-exemption-deals-blow-to-video-game-preservation-efforts.288839/)
- **Internet Archive.** The legal case found was over audio, not software. In *UMG Recordings v. Internet Archive* (the Great 78 Project, over 400,000 shellac records), Universal, Sony and Concord sought about $621m, later updated to about $693m in statutory damages. The parties reached a confidential settlement in September 2025, and the dismissal was granted on 16 October 2025 — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_Music_Group_v._Internet_Archive); [TorrentFreak](https://torrentfreak.com/internet-archive-vs-music-labels-693m-copyright-battle-ends-with-confidential-settlement/)
- **The Strong National Museum of Play / ICHEG (Rochester, NY).** A third-party listing gives over 30,000 games and consoles, including over 20,000 video game titles, plus over 30,000 artefacts and papers. The figure is not confirmed on the museum's own site — [Atlas Obscura](https://www.atlasobscura.com/places/national-center-history-electronic-games). It collects source code. Its 2014 Atari coin-op acquisition (22 pallets) included source code for dozens of games — [Giant Bomb](https://giantbomb.com/articles/a-museum-just-acquired-22-pallets-of-atari-history/1100-4905/). The SSI donation from Joel Billings included company documents, source code and games — [GOG forum, 2013](https://static01.gog.com/forum/general_archive/strategic_simulations_ssi_company_collection_donated_to_museum). See also [Library Journal's archives deep dive](https://www.libraryjournal.com/story/gaming-history-at-the-strong-national-museum-of-play-archives-deep-dive).
- **Computerspielemuseum (Berlin).** Sources disagree on its size. Wikipedia gives more than 37,000 items, including about 25,000 game storage media and about 12,000 magazines — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computerspielemuseum). The museum's own presentation gives about 25,000 software objects and about 4,500 hardware objects — [Computerspielemuseum presentation (Coding da Vinci PDF)](https://codingdavinci.de/sites/default/files/2020-08/computerspielemuseum_berlin.pdf). It is often called the first videogame museum in the world (it opened in 1997; the date is from memory, without a source and is not verified here).
- **National Videogame Museum (Sheffield, UK).** It says it preserves over 5,000 objects of videogame heritage and has over 100 playable exhibits — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Videogame_Museum_(United_Kingdom)); [Welcome to Sheffield](https://www.welcometosheffield.co.uk/content/attractions/the-national-videogame-museum/)
- **Embracer Games Archive (Karlstad, Sweden).** At launch (2021–22) it reported over 50,000 games, consoles and accessories. The oldest items are Magnavox Odyssey games (1972). It began from CEO Lars Wingefors's own collection, and David Boström is its CEO. It is not open to visitors, and Embracer said it planned researcher access. No 2025–26 update was found — [Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2021/05/31/embracer-group-building-archive-of-every-video-game-ever-made); [TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2022/05/30/embracer-games-archive-collection/)
- **Game Preservation Society (Japan).** A non-profit founded in 2011 by Joseph Redon. It focuses on Japanese computer games on floppy disk (PC-88 in particular). Redon estimates it holds 60 to 70 percent of PC-88 games. Kotaku reported over 13,000 early Japanese computer games. Time Extension and Vice reported that it was close to running out of money; the date of those reports was not confirmed — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/saving-japans-games-1819339226); [Time Extension](https://timeextension.com/features/we-might-be-about-to-lose-a-powerful-force-in-the-world-of-video-game-preservation); [Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-game-preservation-society-is-in-danger-of-shutting-down/)

### Inferences
- The institutions keep the *evidence around* games (objects, documents, code). Running the games is left to emulation or to on-site exhibits. US law blocks remote playable access for research, so playability outside museums mostly comes from emulation, re-releases and community projects.
- Source code turns up in institutional collections (The Strong) but is usually not public. That leaves a role for community reimplementations, which rebuild behaviour in public, open code.

### Gaps
- Library of Congress (its game holdings and any role in the DMCA process), MoMA's collection (the 2012 acquisition of 14 games is from memory, without a source, not verified), and the Internet Archive's software collections (MS-DOS and console libraries, run in the browser through emulation) were not researched in this pass.
- Current, museum-confirmed collection sizes for The Strong and Embracer were not found.

## Emulation as preservation

### Takeaway
MAME frames emulation as *documentation of hardware*: its source code is the record, and playability only checks that record. Dump groups and flux imaging were not researched in this pass.

### Cited Findings
- MAME's standard self-description, repeated in its upstream README and in many mirrors and package listings, says it keeps software from disappearing by "documenting the hardware and how it functions." It says "the source code to MAME serves as this documentation," and that the fact that the software is usable "serves primarily to validate the accuracy of the documentation." After absorbing MESS it also covers computers, consoles and calculators — [mirror of the MAME README (wolfmame)](https://github.com/mahlemiut/wolfmame); [Haiku depot listing](https://depot.haiku-os.org/__multipage/pkg/mame). The text was not confirmed on mamedev.org itself.
- The VGHF/SPN exemption request concerned breaking copy protection so that institutions can lend games. It shows that the legal limit on emulation-based access is anti-circumvention (section 1201), not emulation itself — [VGHF statement](https://gamehistory.org/dmca-2024-statement/)

### Inferences
- MAME's framing ("the code is the documentation; running it validates it") is the same framing a reimplementation of one game can use: the new code documents the original game's behaviour, and parity tests show it is accurate.

### Gaps
- No sources were gathered in this pass for higan/ares and cycle accuracy, Redump or No-Intro methods and counts (the Redump wiki refused the connection), or Kryoflux, Greaseweazle and flux imaging.
- No sourced material was found on the limits of emulation for 1995–2010 Windows PC games (Direct3D/Glide, Windows version dependence). This should be researched separately, or framed as an inference only.

## Community reimplementations and source ports

### Takeaway
Almost every project found requires the player's original data and ships no assets. Most describe themselves as a "re-implementation" or "port" and do not use the word "preservation" in their READMEs. Official recognition varies: OpenTTD's Steam/GOG listing came under a 2026 agreement with Atari; ScummVM is used commercially but its site claims no partnership; re3/reVC were removed after a Take-Two lawsuit.

### Cited Findings
- **ScummVM.** It describes itself as "a program which allows you to run certain classic graphical adventure and role-playing games," and says it "is a complete rewrite of these games' executables and is not an emulator." It supports "over 325 adventure games." ScummVM 2026.4.0 ("Never-slop") came out on 11 October 2026 for the 25th anniversary of the project's first public commit by Ludvig Strigeus ("ludde"). That release lists 194 newly supported games and 12 new engines. Original data files are needed. The front and About pages do not use the word "preservation" — [scummvm.org](https://www.scummvm.org/); [scummvm.org/about](https://www.scummvm.org/about/)
- ScummVM and GOG: a 2014 forum remark by a ScummVM team member said the project gets no money from games sold with ScummVM, only affiliate commission. This is informal and old — [GamingOnLinux comments, 2014](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2014/10/gog-announces-20--lucasarts-classics-to-release-on-the-digital-platform/page=1/). The ScummVM About page names no GOG or Steam partnership; its only commercial link is a ZOOM Platform affiliate link — [scummvm.org/about](https://www.scummvm.org/about/)
- **OpenTTD.** It arrived on Steam on 1 April 2021, free, with a Linux build — [GamingOnLinux](https://gamingonlinux.com/2021/04/the-excellent-business-and-building-sim-openttd-arrives-on-steam). In mid-March 2026 Atari re-released Transport Tycoon Deluxe on Steam and GOG. On 14 March 2026 OpenTTD announced that new players on Steam/GOG must buy TTD; existing owners keep access, and the official website and package managers stay free. A 19 March update said: "we have not been 'pressured' by Atari," that "a compromise would be needed to balance Atari's commercial interests," and that Atari contributes to infrastructure costs — [rubenwardy blog summary](https://blog.rubenwardy.com/2026/03/17/openttd-ttd-release/); official posts at [openttd.org 14 March](https://www.openttd.org/news/2026/03/14/steam-changes) and [openttd.org 19 March](https://www.openttd.org/news/2026/03/19/steam-changes-update) (quotes taken from the blog, not checked against the originals); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/openttd/)
- **OpenRCT2.** "An open-source re-implementation of RollerCoaster Tycoon 2," originally "written by Chris Sawyer in x86 assembly." "OpenRCT2 requires original files of RollerCoaster Tycoon 2 to play" (it points to Steam and GOG) — [GitHub README](https://github.com/OpenRCT2/OpenRCT2)
- **OpenLoco.** "An open source re-implementation of Chris Sawyer's Locomotion." The README says the rewrite in C++ is finished and the aims now include multiplayer and higher limits. It no longer shares a codebase with OpenRCT2. It "requires the asset files of the original Chris Sawyer's Locomotion" — [GitHub README](https://github.com/OpenLoco/OpenLoco)
- **DevilutionX.** "A port of Diablo and Hellfire that strives to make it simple to run the game." "You'll need access to the data from the original game" (DIABDAT.MPQ; shareware spawn.mpq also works). It links to GOG as a place to buy and has a wiki guide for extracting files from the GOG installer — [GitHub README](https://github.com/diasurgical/devilutionX)
- **The Force Engine.** Its goal is "to reverse engineer and rebuild the 'Jedi Engine' for modern systems." Dark Forces is fully supported, with mods; Outlaws is planned for TFE 2.0. "A purchased copy of the original game is required" — [GitHub README](https://github.com/luciusDXL/TheForceEngine)
- **Fallout 2 Community Edition.** "A fully working re-implementation of Fallout 2… that works (mostly) hassle-free on multiple platforms." "You must own the game to play" (master.dat, critter.dat, patch000.dat, data/) — [GitHub README](https://github.com/alexbatalov/fallout2-ce)
- **dethrace (Carmageddon).** "An attempt to learn how the 1997 driving/mayhem game Carmageddon works behind the scenes." It works by "slowly replacing the original assembly code with equivalent C code, function by function." Debug symbols found on the Splat Pack CD "did not match any known released executable" but helped with names. It credits `reccmp` for matching. It needs "the data from the original game" (CD, GOG or freeware demos) — [GitHub README](https://github.com/dethrace-labs/dethrace)
- **OpenMW.** 0.49.0 came out on 4 July 2025 after about three years: a larger Lua API, animation and lighting polish, and the first release that goes beyond Morrowind. 0.50.0 came out on 7 November 2025 (gamepad support, first steps in dehardcoding combat) — [openmw.org 0.49.0](https://openmw.org/2025/openmw-0-49-0-released/); [GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2025/02/morrowind-game-engine-openmw-gearing-up-for-a-huge-new-0-49-release)
- **re3 / reVC (GTA III / Vice City).** Engines rebuilt by reverse engineering. Take-Two's first DMCA notice (2021) was reversed after a counter-notice. Take-Two then sued in California, also citing the EULA, and GitHub removed the repositories again. The developers argued fair use: "we are improving and fixing the game as well as bringing it to new platforms." No final judgment or settlement was found — [GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2021/11/gta-modders-behind-re3-and-revc-fire-back-in-court); [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/take-two-gta-reverse-engineering-project-takedown/); [DSOGaming](https://www.dsogaming.com/news/take-two-has-filled-a-lawsuit-against-the-team-that-reverse-engineered-gta3-gta-vice-city/)
- **Nightdive Studios and community developers.** Doom Wiki lists Doom community members employed at Nightdive (Samuel "Kaiser" Villarreal, James "Quasar" Haley, Edward "Edward850" Richardson, Max "Altazimuth" Waine). It says Villarreal's Doom 64 EX and Powerslave EX work led to the KEX engine. Atari announced its acquisition of Nightdive on 22 March 2023 — [Doom Wiki](https://doomwiki.org/wiki/Nightdive_Studios); [Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2023/03/23/atari-night-dive-studios-acquisition-system-shock)

### Inferences
- "Bring your own data" is the near-universal rule. It is what separates reimplementation from piracy, and it is what lets stores (Steam, GOG) sell the original as the way in. OpenTTD's 2026 arrangement makes that link explicit: the original game is bought to play the reimplementation on a store.
- The READMEs found avoid the word "preservation" and describe the projects as ports, re-implementations or engine rebuilds, with the aim of running on modern systems. Preservation framing comes mostly from journalists and institutions. A site should present preservation as the *effect* of these projects, not as their stated aim, unless it quotes a source that says so.
- The re3 case shows the legal risk: a project that matches the original code closely and targets a still-sold, high-value franchise drew a lawsuit. The projects that thrive mostly target older titles, often with publisher tolerance or an agreement.

### Gaps
- GemRB, OpenDUNE and OpenMW's own mission statement were not fetched.
- The extent of GOG bundling of ScummVM, DOSBox or DevilutionX with store releases was not confirmed from a primary source. GOG does ship ScummVM with many adventure games (memory, without a source, unverified here). GOG's "Preservation Program" (launched 2024) was not researched.
- No primary source was found for Nightdive directly building on a community source port (for example, using a fan engine in a commercial release).

## Matching decompilation and static recompilation

### Takeaway
Matching decompilations (SM64 and others) produce source that rebuilds byte-identical binaries and need the original ROM for assets. Static recompilation (N64Recomp) turns machine code into C mechanically, needs only the ROM, and enabled Zelda 64: Recompiled (Majora's Mask, May 2024). Both are framed mainly around ports and modding; journalists add the preservation angle.

### Cited Findings
- Matching decompilation means source that compiles to the target machine code with a specified toolchain. Functional reconstruction aims only to match behaviour — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/features/decompilations-could-be-the-solution-to-ports-and-remakes-in-the-future)
- The n64decomp/sm64 repository covers the JP, US, EU, Shindou and iQue releases. It does not include all assets needed to build the ROMs, so the original ROM is required — [GitHub n64decomp/sm64](https://github.com/n64decomp/sm64)
- TechRadar notes that game preservation "has grown in importance" and presents decompilations as a way to keep retro games running on modern systems — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/features/decompilations-could-be-the-solution-to-ports-and-remakes-in-the-future)
- N64Recomp (Mr-Wiseguy) statically recompiles N64 machine code into C. Unlike a decompilation, the output is not readable, and only the original ROM is needed to run it. Zelda 64: Recompiled (Majora's Mask) uses the RT64 renderer and adds ultrawide, high frame rates and ray tracing. The port was reportedly built in about two days, helped by the existing decompilation. Wiseguy also made recompiled ports of Banjo-Kazooie, Rocket: Robot on Wheels and Superman 64 — [VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/unofficial-pc-ports-of-n64-games-could-be-about-to-get-a-lot-easier); [Time Extension](https://www.timeextension.com/news/2024/05/new-tool-allows-n64-games-to-be-played-with-ray-tracing-uncapped-frame-rates-and-ultrawide-support); [Nintendo Life / Digital Foundry](https://www.nintendolife.com/news/2024/05/video-digital-foundry-takes-a-look-at-zelda-64-recompiled-majoras-mask)

### Inferences
- There are three approaches, in rising order of readability: static recompilation (mechanical, not readable), matching decompilation (readable, byte-exact), and clean reimplementation (readable, behaviour-exact, free in structure). Each ships no original assets and needs the player's own copy.
- Recompilation leaned on an existing decompilation. Documentation work compounds: one project's understanding speeds up the next.

### Gaps
- pret (pokered and others) READMEs were not fetched, and nor were the zeldaret OoT/MM statements of purpose.
- Nintendo's stance is reported only as "no legal action against matching decomps" from secondary commentary; no primary statement was found.

## Documentation of game behaviour beyond playability

### Takeaway
Little was gathered. The sources found suggest that documentation is itself preservation: MAME treats code as documentation, and dethrace's stated aim is "to learn how [the game] works." Wikis such as ModdingWiki and The Cutting Room Floor were not reached in a usable form.

### Cited Findings
- MAME: "the source code to MAME serves as this documentation"; usability validates it — [MAME README mirror](https://github.com/mahlemiut/wolfmame)
- dethrace describes itself first as an attempt "to learn how… Carmageddon works behind the scenes" — [GitHub README](https://github.com/dethrace-labs/dethrace)
- The VGHF library preserves development assets and ephemera as research material without playable access — [Time Extension](https://timeextension.com/news/2025/01/the-video-game-history-foundation-digital-library-is-now-available-in-early-access)

### Inferences
- A reimplementation leaves two durable outputs: playable code, and a written record of formats and mechanics. The record survives even when the code is taken down (re3 shows that code can be removed).

### Gaps
- The Cutting Room Floor's About page gave no usable description, article count or founding date.
- ModdingWiki (Shikadi), speedrun communities, and format documentation inside projects (for example the OpenRCT2 or OpenMW wikis) were not researched.
